## 1. Reporting standards, mapped to the check groups

The guidelines that fit the theses this vault sees:

- **Surveys** (cross-sectional questionnaires): JARS Table 1 and Table 3 (nonexperimental), STROBE cross-sectional, CROSS; CHERRIES when the survey is online.
- **Quasi-experiments** (one-group or two-group pre/post, no randomisation): JARS Table 2 with Module B, TREND. CONSORT 2025 applies only to randomised trials. JARS routes "experimental manipulation without randomization" to Table 2 and Module B (JARS Table 1, *Conditions and Design*).
- **System development** (an information system, tool, or calculator, then its evaluation): no EQUATOR guideline covers building an artefact. Design science research gives the process: problem identification, objectives for a solution, design and development, demonstration, evaluation, communication (DSRM abstract). STARE-HI covers reporting the evaluation of a health-informatics system.

## 2. What examiners weigh that the checks miss

**What the sources found.**

- Examiners' reports are dominated by *analysis and reporting*: it appears in 89% of reports and averages about 43% of a report's text. Design and methods are mentioned in 60% of reports but take only 7% of the text; ethics are "barely mentioned" (Holbrook-a, p. 136).
- The literature is treated as "a critical indicator of the quality of the project". A review that is "a listing of known facts", with no interpretation and no conceptual framework for the research, is correlated with a recommendation for revision or failure (Holbrook-a, pp. 133–134).
- Contribution and significance are mentioned in 70% of reports, usually in a few words (Holbrook-a, p. 130). Bourke & Holbrook's 12 quality indicators span contribution, the literature review, approach and methodology, analyses and results, and presentation, and together they are "strongly related to the holistic assessment of thesis quality" (B&H, abstract).
- Editorial comment appears in half the reports but averages 8% of the text (Holbrook-a, p. 136). Holbrook-a reports, citing Mullins & Kiley, that "a poorly written thesis generally had a negative effect on the examiner, suggesting disengagement" (Holbrook-a, p. 127).
- Mullins & Kiley interviewed 30 experienced examiners about the criteria they use and their "critical judgement points" (M&K, abstract). As reported by Holbrook-a (p. 127): examiners distinguish clearly between poor, acceptable and outstanding theses, go in expecting the student to pass, and use criteria of their own rather than the guidelines they are given. These three points are secondhand: the full text of M&K was not reachable.
- The most useful examiner comment — Holbrook's *formative instruction* — combines commentary, "detail about how to proceed", and engagement with the topic. Advice "without adequate context or structure", or comment so vague it cannot be acted on, is the kind committees "dismiss from their deliberations" (Holbrook-a, pp. 137–138).

**What it means for the critique.** The current groups check numbers, presence, design, ethics and references. They do not judge the literature review's synthesis or the stated contribution, which is where examiners say a thesis fails.

| # | Candidate check | Source | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | The literature review builds the conceptual framework it ends with: each variable or dimension in the framework traces to reviewed sources, rather than the review listing studies one after another. | Holbrook-a, pp. 133–134 | awaiting a real defect |

The finding shape that #16 proposes — *evidence / effect / change to* — is the shape Holbrook-a calls complete formative instruction: commentary (evidence and effect), direction (change to), engaged with the specific text. That supports the layout; it is not a new check.

## 3. Rubric design — for issue #17 to cite

**What a rubric is.** "A rubric is a coherent set of criteria for student work that describes levels of performance quality" (Brookhart-13, ASCD page). "Rubrics, checklists, and rating scales all have criteria; the scale is what distinguishes them. Checklists ask for dichotomous decisions … Rating scales ask for decisions across a scale that does not describe the performance" (Brookhart-18, Introduction). Brookhart-18's review classifies separately the tools whose level descriptions "included rating-scale language and/or relied on counting occurrences" (Table 1).

→ For #17 rule 1: a criterion worth 8 marks whose scores 1–7 have no description is, by Brookhart's definition, a rating scale on that criterion, not a rubric.

**Descriptors.**

- "General rubrics should be written with descriptive language, as opposed to evaluative language (e.g., excellent, poor)", because descriptive language shows the student where they are and where to go next (Brookhart-18). An example of the descriptive kind, from a rubric for research theses: "All elements are logically connected … Research questions, hypotheses, research design, results, inferences and evaluations are related and form a consistent and concise argumentation" (Prins, quoted in Brookhart-18).
- The language describing a criterion should stay consistent across the scale levels: each level describes the same attributes (T&S, abstract).
- Criteria must be substantive: "If the criterion is simply having or counting something", rubrics "misdirect student efforts and mis-measure learning" (Brookhart-18).

**How many levels.** "The number of levels should be related to the types of decisions that need to be made and to the number of reliable distinctions in student work that are possible and helpful" (Brookhart-18). In the 51 higher-education rubrics Brookhart reviewed, four levels (14) and five (8) were the most common; "three, four, and five are the most common number of levels" (Brookhart-18, Results, Conclusions). The VALUE rubrics use four: Capstone 4, Milestones 3 and 2, Benchmark 1, and "evaluators are encouraged to assign a zero to any work sample … that does not meet benchmark (cell one) level performance" (VALUE).

Giving every criterion the same number of levels is itself a risk. Humphry & Heldsinger present "empirical evidence for … a potentially widespread threat to the validity of rubric assessments that arose due to design features", and question "whether rubrics with structurally aligned categories can validly assess complex skills"; their remedy is to rethink the rubric's structure (H&H, abstract). The full text, with the details of the redesign, was not reachable.

**How a score within a level is assigned.** No source read here gives a procedure for awarding different points inside one described level.

- Dawson names the *scoring strategy* — how a mark is reached — as one of fourteen design elements that make one rubric different from another, alongside quality levels, quality definitions, and *secrecy* (who sees the rubric, and when) (Dawson, abstract; the element list as given in Brookhart-18).
- The examples found map each level to one score. VALUE scores a level as its number, 0–4. The NU thesis rubric (2026) scores each criterion as *weight × band / 4*, with four described bands (Excellent 4, Good 3, Adequate 2, Improvement needed 1), and the band used is the average of the committee members' bands (NU, *Instruction*).
- Brookhart-18: "If all that is required is a grading scheme, traditional point schemes or rating scales are easier for instructors to use."

→ For #17 rule 1, the sourced position is: each score a criterion can award either is a described level, or is computed by a stated rule from described parts (a sum of sub-parts, or weight × level). A range of points inside one level, chosen by the marker, has no descriptor of its own and is the rating-scale case above. This is an inference from the definitions, not a finding any source states.

→ For #17 rule 2, Dawson's *secrecy* and *scoring strategy* elements are the vocabulary: a summary the examinee receives that shows totals without the strategy that produces them keeps the scoring strategy secret.

**Counter-examples from real forms**, useful as test cases for the defect wording:

- NRRU EFT07 scores each item 5 = ดีมาก … 1 = ควรปรับปรุงอย่างยิ่ง: evaluative labels with no description of the work — a rating scale under Brookhart's definition.
- CU-Env scores sub-items out of 5 or 10 (e.g. ความคิดริเริ่มสร้างสรรค์ 10) with no level descriptions — a point scheme; a 7 out of 10 has no source.

`skills/make-exam/ASSESSMENT-REFERENCE.md` and `skills/make-practical/PRACTICAL-REFERENCE.md` (*Rubrics*) already require observable criteria, distinguishable levels, and "typically 3–5 levels". The sources above support those lines and add two things for #17 to consider: descriptive rather than evaluative wording (Brookhart-18), and the same attributes described at every level (T&S).

## 4. Examiner report formats

**Thai forms read.**

- **NU (graduate school, master's thesis exam).** One outcome per committee: S (pass with distinction, with or without revisions within *n* days), S (pass, with or without revisions within *n* days), U (fail). Pass needs 70% on thesis quality and 70% on the oral; distinction 90%. Then an analytic rubric per examiner, five weighted criteria with four described bands, and a free-text box ข้อคิดเห็นเพิ่มเติม. No prescribed structure for the comments.
- **CU-Env (doctoral proposal).** Two separate pages per examiner: a score sheet kept by the department (content 50, document 15, presentation 15, answers 25; pass > 60), and a free-text page ข้อเสนอแนะเพิ่มเติม "for the student to revise". The proposal is judged as submitted, not as revised after submission. One examiner's fail fails the exam.
- **NRRU (proposal, undergraduate special problem).** A 1–5 rating per section of the proposal and per aspect of the presentation, with cut-offs (85–100% very good … below 60% fail). No space for findings.

**International.**

- **Bristol.** Each examiner writes a preliminary report before the oral. The joint final report must "make a recommendation, provide a supporting rationale, and include guidance" on corrections. The guidance is merged across examiners, "specifies the parts of the work where improvement is required", is not optional in tone, and bounds how far the changes may go. Minor errors (typing, small additions to text, references or diagrams) are kept apart from errors of substance; many minor errors together can count as substantial. Outcomes run from A (award unconditionally) through B (minor errors, 28 days), C (errors of substance, six months), D (resubmit, twelve months) to H (fail) (Bristol 22.8–22.24).
- **Holbrook-a**: "Often the examiner will have a section of their report labelled typographical errors or editorial comment", kept apart from the substance; editorial matters average 8% of a report's text (p. 135).

**What they share.** Every form puts the verdict first, in the institution's outcome categories. Bristol separates what *must* change, by part of the work, from editorial corrections, and requires a rationale. None of the Thai forms prescribes how a finding is written.

### Recommendation for the critique layout (for #16 to adopt or reject)

1. **Summary first, with the verdict in the host institution's own outcome words** (e.g. ผ่าน / ผ่านโดยมีเงื่อนไข / ไม่ผ่าน, or the NU S/U scale), and the rationale in two or three sentences. Every form read leads with the outcome; Bristol requires the rationale with it.
2. **Then one table of what to change** — finding code, page, what to change, and whether it must be fixed before the exam or should be fixed. This is Bristol's merged corrections guidance "specifying the parts of the work", and what the students act on.
3. **Each finding under the fixed sub-heads *evidence / effect / change to*.** It matches Holbrook-a's complete formative instruction and avoids the vague comment committees discard.
4. **Formatting and typing as their own table, after the substance** — Bristol's minor-errors category and the editorial section examiners already write; kept apart so typing does not crowd out the findings, and so many of them can still be flagged as substantial when they are.
5. **Strengths, then sources used to check**, as now.

The current *Reporting* order in `THESIS-CHECKS.md` already follows points 1, 3 (in part), 4 and 5; the change is point 2 — the table of changes after the summary — and naming the verdict in the institution's categories.
