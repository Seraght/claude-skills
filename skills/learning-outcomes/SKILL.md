# Learning Outcomes

What an outcome has to be before anything can be built on it, and the two directions it is checked in. The `design-course` skill writes outcomes against this file; `make-exam` checks them against it before building a blueprint. This file is reference only — it carries no steps of its own.

**The host project governs.** A programme, an institution, or a country imposes its own outcome framework and its own forms; where one exists it governs, and this file fills the gaps it leaves. What follows is not a standard — it is what the standards have in common.

## Constructive alignment

Three things are designed together, and the middle one is what makes the other two true:

```
Learning outcome  ──►  Teaching and learning  ──►  Assessment
 (what it claims)      (the chance to do it)      (whether they can)
```

They carry the same verb at the same level. An outcome saying *design* that is taught by lecture and assessed by recalling design principles is not a strict outcome imperfectly served — it is a different outcome, and the marks say so. Alignment is the property being checked, in both directions:

- **Vertically** — each course outcome serves at least one programme outcome, and each programme outcome the course is assigned reaches at least one course outcome. An outcome serving nothing upward is the course's own invention; a programme outcome nothing reaches is a hole in the curriculum map, and it is the map that is wrong, not the course.
- **Horizontally** — each outcome has teaching hours, an assessment that elicits its verb, and a share of the marks. Any of the three missing makes the outcome decorative.

## What an outcome must contain

The mandatory part is a **behaviour**: an observable verb and the content it acts on. Two parts are conditional:

| | Part | Include it when |
|---|---|---|
| **B** | Observable verb + content | **Always.** Without it there is nothing to assess |
| **C** | Condition — what is supplied, and under what constraints | The condition changes the difficulty: with a case supplied, without notes, within a time limit |
| **D** | Degree — how well is good enough | The outcome will decide pass or fail, or a rubric level has to be named |

Mager's original demand is the one that still does the work: use *doing words* — verbs whose performance can be seen — and never *know*, *learn*, *appreciate*, *be aware of*. The audience ("students will be able to…") is conventional and carries no information.

## The four ways a verb fails

Each is a defect with a fix, and each is cheap now and expensive after the paper is written.

- **Compound.** Two verbs in one outcome — "create X **and** evaluate Y", "analyse A **and** identify B" — usually at two different cognitive levels. No single assessment serves it, so the assessment becomes two assessments sharing one outcome's marks, and it reads that way to anyone sitting it. *Fix:* split into two outcomes. The marks and the hours then divide by outcome instead of being argued about inside one.
- **Unobservable.** *Understand*, *know*, *realise*, *appreciate* name a state, not a performance. There is no item that confirms them, so in practice something else gets assessed and the outcome is decorative. *Fix:* ask what the learner would **do** to show it, and make that the verb.
- **Ambiguous.** A verb that sits at two levels depending on reading — *explain* (restating a cause is Understand; working out why is Analyze), *compare* (against criteria given is Understand; choosing the criteria is Analyze). *Fix:* the condition disambiguates it. "Compare against the criteria supplied" and "compare, choosing your own criteria" are different outcomes.
- **Inflated.** The verb claims a level the course never teaches to and the paper never reaches. *Fix:* raise the teaching and the assessment, or lower the verb. Reading the verb loosely so the existing paper qualifies is the one move that is not available — that is how a course states an outcome it does not deliver, in writing, for as long as the syllabus stands.

## What can evidence which level

An outcome is only as assessable as the formats available to it. Choosing the format at writing time rather than at design time is what quietly caps a course at its lowest level.

| Level | Reached by |
|---|---|
| Remember, Understand | Selected-response and matching items, short answer |
| Apply, Analyze | Items built on a situation the learner has not already seen resolved; problems; structured short answer |
| Evaluate, Create | Constructed response, a product, a performance or practical station — each with a rubric |

No selected-response item evidences Create, whatever its stem says. An outcome at that level with no constructed-response, product, or performance behind it is unassessed however the marks total.

## How many

For a full-weight course, roughly four to six. This is practice rather than a standard — no standards body names a number — and the reasoning is arithmetic: a term divided by five outcomes leaves enough weeks per outcome to teach it, practise it, and assess it. Fewer than three and the outcomes are too broad to attach marks to; more than seven and some of them are certainly not being assessed.

## Three rules for the alignment table

The table that pairs each outcome with its teaching, its assessment, and its marks is where misalignment becomes visible. Three things in it are always defects:

1. **The verb changes along the row.** An outcome saying *design* taught as *design* but assessed by a written test about design principles is misaligned by definition, not by degree.
2. **A block of marks with no outcome.** Marks awarded for something no outcome claims — participation, neatness, effort — are either an outcome that has not been written down, or marks that should not exist.
3. **A high-level outcome carrying trivial weight.** An outcome at *design* or *evaluate* worth 5% beside a *remember* outcome worth 60% describes a course that says one thing and rewards another. Learners follow the marks.

## Checking a set of outcomes

Run this over the whole set, not one outcome at a time — most of these are properties of the set:

- Every outcome passes the four verb checks.
- Every outcome maps upward to at least one programme outcome, and every programme outcome the course is assigned is reached.
- Every outcome has teaching hours behind it, and every scheduled session serves an outcome or is marked as not assessed.
- Every outcome has an assessment whose format can reach its level, and a share of the marks.
- The marks sum to the course's total, and their distribution across levels matches what the outcomes claim.
- Where the programme has more than one revision in force, the outcomes name which revision they map to. A course taught to two cohorts under two revisions maps to both, separately.
