# Assessment Reference

General assessment principles the `make-exam` skill applies. When the host project carries its own assessment reference, that one governs and this file fills the gaps it leaves.

## Cognitive levels (Bloom's revised taxonomy)

Remember → Understand → Apply → Analyze → Evaluate → Create.

Tag every item with the level it actually demands, judged by what the examinee must *do*, not by the verb in the stem: an item asking to "analyze" a case whose answer was stated verbatim in the notes tests Remember. A paper weighted entirely at the lower two levels cannot evidence a higher-level outcome no matter how many items it holds.

## Blueprint

The blueprint is a table fixed *before* any item is written: rows are topics, columns are cognitive levels, cells hold item counts and marks.

- **Weight by instructional time.** A topic's share of the marks tracks its share of the teaching hours, taken from the actual course document — never estimated.
- **Map every cell to a learning outcome.** An outcome with no items is unassessed; items serving no outcome are cut.
- **Match level to outcome.** The blueprint's level distribution reflects the levels the outcomes state.
- Total marks reconcile with the grade weighting in the course document before item writing starts.

## Item writing

- **The stem carries the question.** A reader should know what is being asked before reaching the options.
- **Options are homogeneous and plausible.** Same grammatical form, similar length; every distractor represents a real misconception, so a knowledgeable examinee rejects it for a reason rather than on sight.
- **Keep cueing out.** Grammatical agreement, a conspicuously longer correct option, absolutes ("always", "never") in distractors, and overlapping options all leak the answer.
- **One item, one point.** Items stay independent — answering item 7 never depends on getting item 6 right.
- **Constructed-response items state the expected scope** — length, format, and what a complete answer covers — so the rubric can be applied consistently.

## Answer key

Every item gets a key. For selected-response items, record why each distractor is wrong: the rationale is what makes the item defensible when an examinee challenges it, and it drives revision after item analysis.

## Rubrics

For constructed-response and practical items.

- **Analytic** (separate criteria, each scored) for diagnostic feedback and multi-marker consistency; **holistic** (one overall judgement) for speed on short responses. Analytic is the default when more than one person marks.
- **Criteria are observable.** Each level descriptor names what is present or absent in the work, not how good it felt.
- **Levels are distinguishable.** Adjacent levels differ by something a marker can point at; typically 3–5 levels.
- **Criterion weights sum to the item's marks**, and reflect what the outcome actually values.

## Practical exams

- Assess the performance, not a written account of it: score what is observed while the examinee works.
- Define the station's conditions — equipment, time limit, permitted references, safety constraints — as part of the item, since they change what the score means.
- Use a checklist for procedural steps with a right order, and a rubric for judgement-based aspects; most practical stations need both.
- Where safety or a critical error voids the attempt, state that rule in the rubric rather than leaving it to marker discretion.

## Item analysis (after administration)

- **Difficulty (p)** — proportion answering correctly. Items near 0.5 discriminate best; near 0 or 1 they separate nobody, though a deliberate easy opener or a mastery item is legitimate.
- **Discrimination (D)** — whether high scorers outperform low scorers on the item. Zero or negative discrimination flags an item to revise or retire: usually a miskeyed answer, an ambiguous stem, or two defensible options.
- Feed the findings back into the item bank so the next paper starts from evidence rather than from scratch.
