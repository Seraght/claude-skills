# Constructed-Response Defects

Defects that essay, short-answer, and problem items carry. The defects that apply to items of every kind are in [COMMON-DEFECTS.md](./COMMON-DEFECTS.md), and every item is judged against that file as well as this one.

A constructed-response item is inseparable from its rubric: the prompt and the scoring guide are one instrument, and a defect in either distorts the score. An item with no rubric is not an item that will be marked generously — it is an item that will be marked differently by each marker, and differently by the same marker on Friday than on Monday.

**The host project governs.** Where a project or an institution fixes a rule — a required response length, a mandated form — an item is not flawed for following it.

## Group A — marks an unprepared examinee can still earn

- **Bluffing pays.** The rubric rewards length, the number of points raised, or fluency, so an examinee who writes at length around the topic scores near one who answers it. *Fix:* anchor each level on what the response must *contain*, and cap the marks a response can earn without the required content.
- **Answerable from general knowledge.** The prompt can be answered acceptably by a literate adult who never took the course. *Fix:* require the course's own concepts, data, or vocabulary in the response, and say so in the rubric.
- **The prompt hands over its own structure.** A prompt that lists the parts of the answer ("explain the cause, the effect, and the prevention") lets an examinee earn structural marks by restating the three headings. *Fix:* keep the structural demand in the rubric where it is scored, not in the prompt where it is given away — or state the parts and score only their content.
- **The stimulus contains the answer.** A case, table, or quotation supplied with the item already states what the examinee is asked to conclude. *Fix:* remove the conclusion from the stimulus, leaving the evidence it was drawn from.

## Group B — irrelevant difficulty

- **Unbounded scope.** The prompt does not state length, format, or what a complete answer covers, so examinees spend their marks guessing how much is wanted. Scores then separate the confident from the careful rather than the able from the unable. *Fix:* state the expected length, the form, and the number of points or aspects required.
- **Optional questions.** Examinees choose three prompts from five. The prompts are not of equal difficulty, nothing equates them, and the resulting scores are not comparable between examinees — a defect no marking care can repair. *Fix:* give every examinee the same items; where breadth of choice is the goal, vary the context inside one prompt instead.
- **Several questions, one mark.** A prompt asking for a definition, an example, and a critique, scored as a single judgement, cannot distinguish which part failed. *Fix:* split into separately scored criteria, or into separate items.
- **Reading load out of proportion to the marks.** A two-page case for a four-mark question spends the examinee's time where the marks are not. *Fix:* cut the stimulus to what the answer needs, or raise the marks to match.
- **Language demand beyond the construct.** Outside a language course, marks lost to spelling, handwriting, or phrasing measure something the outcome never claimed. *Fix:* say in the rubric that mechanics are not scored, unless the outcome names them.

## Group C — congruence, level, and scoring

- **No rubric, or an unobservable one.** Level descriptors reading "ครบถ้วน", "ดีมาก", or "shows good understanding" name a marker's impression, not a feature of the work. Two markers will not agree, and neither will one marker with themselves. *Fix:* every descriptor names what is present or absent in the response.
- **Adjacent levels a marker cannot separate.** If the difference between 3 and 4 marks cannot be pointed at in the work, the item scores at 3-or-4 resolution while claiming to score at 1. *Fix:* rewrite until each boundary names something observable; cut a level where none can be found.
- **Weights that do not sum to the item's marks**, or that sit on the criterion easiest to write rather than on what the outcome values. *Fix:* recompute from the outcome; the criterion carrying the outcome's verb carries the most marks.
- **A score with no source.** A criterion awards scores that no descriptor reaches: worth 8, with descriptors at 8, 4, and 0 and nothing for the scores between, or a bare "out of 10" with no levels at all. Each unsourced score is the marker's impression on a scale, which is a rating scale labelled a rubric, and two markers will not agree on a 7. *Fix:* give every score its own descriptor, or make it the result of a stated rule over described parts — a sum of sub-parts that each have a descriptor, or a weight times a described level — and cut the scores neither can reach.
- **The examinee's copy hides how marks are reached.** The paper, or any criteria summary the examinee receives, shows a total per criterion ("C1 … 8") and nothing on what earns it, or sends the reader to a file they cannot open, such as a path inside the vault. The scoring strategy is then secret, and an examinee cannot aim at what the criterion rewards. *Fix:* give at least one line per criterion on how its marks are reached ("C2 = 4 prompt elements × 1 + revision rounds 0/2/4"), or point to a rubric the examinee actually receives.
- **Level mismatch.** The prompt says "วิเคราะห์" but a recalled definition earns full marks under the rubric. The verb in the prompt is a claim; the rubric is what the item actually measures. *Fix:* raise the rubric to the verb, or lower the verb to the rubric and move the cell in the blueprint.
- **No defence against marker drift.** A judgement-heavy item marked by one person, in script order, with the examinee's name visible, carries halo, order, and fatigue effects that show up as marks. *Fix:* mark question by question across all scripts rather than script by script, anonymise where the institution allows, and calibrate on a shared sample before marking begins.

## Group D — the paper as a whole

- **The writing does not fit the time.** Sum the response lengths the prompts demand against the exam duration and what an examinee can actually write. A paper that cannot be finished measures speed. *Fix:* cut items, or cut the scope each prompt asks for.
- **One outcome resting on one long item.** A single essay carrying a whole outcome scores that outcome once, and one bad question, one misread prompt, or one bad morning takes all of it. *Fix:* split the marks across more than one item, or pair the essay with items of another format serving the same cell.
- **Rubric criteria repeated across items** so the same ability is scored three times and another is never scored. *Fix:* reconcile the criteria against the blueprint, not against each item alone.

## Sources

- Haladyna, Downing & Rodriguez (2002), "A Review of Multiple-Choice Item-Writing Guidelines for Classroom Assessment", *Applied Measurement in Education* 15(3) — the item-format guidance the selected-response taxonomy is drawn from, including the case against optional questions.
- Brookhart (2018), "Appropriate criteria: key to effective rubrics", *Frontiers in Education* 3, 22 — rubrics, checklists, and rating scales differ by their scale: a rating scale asks for decisions across a scale that does not describe the performance, which is what a score with no source is.
- Dawson (2017), "Assessment rubrics: towards clearer and more replicable design, research and practice", *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education* 42(3) — the scoring strategy and its secrecy (who sees the rubric, and when) as design elements of a rubric.
- Jonsson & Svingby (2007), "The use of scoring rubrics: Reliability, validity and educational consequences", *Educational Research Review* 2(2) — what makes rubric scoring reproducible, and what analytic criteria with behavioural anchors buy over holistic judgement.
